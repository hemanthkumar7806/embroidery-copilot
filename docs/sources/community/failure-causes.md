## Too much density (stitch overload)

**Content from source:** “Too many stitches make fabrics pucker or tear. Fix: Trim density by 10–15% and use underlay for better coverage.” “Overly dense stitching increases thread tension and friction, which can cause frequent thread breaks and needle stress. Excess density also makes embroidery stiff and uncomfortable on garments. Reducing stitch density while maintaining adequate coverage improves machine performance and garment flexibility.” “When stitch density is too high, threads pile up and distort, causing rippling and surface deformation. The ideal density for most embroidery is around 0.4mm spacing between parallel stitches—closer spacing forces threads to compete for space and roll over each other.”

**Source:** [DigitizingUSA – Stitch density](https://www.digitizingusa.com/showblog/stitch-density-in-embroidery-digitizing), [Embroiplanet – Common embroidery problems](https://embroiplanet.com/common-embroidery-problems-caused-by-poor-digitizing-and-how-to-fix-them/), community/forums

## No or wrong underlay

**Content from source:** “Without underlay stitches, top stitches may sink into the fabric. This leads to uneven texture, gaps, and weak structure. For fill stitches, use crisscross or grid style underlay. For satin, use center or edge runs.” “Without adequate underlay, fabric can shift and pull, causing puckering and uneven surfaces.” “Without an underlay, embroidery lies flat on the underlying fabric which can often show through. Underlays help stabilize fabrics and reduce puckering and pulling especially on larger designs. It also provides ‘loft’, raising cover stitches, and prevents them from sinking into soft fabrics. On knits, edge runs are best.” “Without proper underlay, embroidery lies flat and can show through. For knits like hoodies, edge run underlay is often best.”

**Source:** [The Embroidery Cloud – Top 7 mistakes](https://theembroiderycloud.com/top-7-common-embroidery-digitizing-mistakes-and-how-to-avoid-them/), [Embroiplanet](https://embroiplanet.com/common-embroidery-problems-caused-by-poor-digitizing-and-how-to-fix-them/), [Hatchembroidery – Underlay](https://hatchembroidery.com/resources/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-underlay-for-your-machine-embroidery-designs), [Hooping Station](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/the-ultimate-embroidery-digitizing-cheat-sheet-density-underlay-pull-comp-and-objects-explained-for-real-stitch-out-results)

## Wrong or missing pull compensation

**Content from source:** “During stitching, threads naturally pull fabric inward, slightly shrinking shapes. If digitizing does not compensate for this pull, circles may become oval and straight lines may curve. Pull compensation adjustments maintain correct logo proportions after stitching.” “Pull Compensation (Pull Comp) intentionally makes the digitizing wider than the final shapes, knowing it will shrink back to the correct size. Never digitize ‘line to line.’ If you want two shapes to touch, they must overlap in the software.” “Pull compensation is typically set at 0.2mm as a default, but 0.4mm is recommended for use with automatic digitizing. Range 0.1–0.4mm depending on density, fabric type, thread tension, and element thickness.”

**Source:** [Embroiplanet – Common embroidery problems](https://embroiplanet.com/common-embroidery-problems-caused-by-poor-digitizing-and-how-to-fix-them/), [Hooping Station – Pull comp](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/the-ultimate-embroidery-digitizing-cheat-sheet-density-underlay-pull-comp-and-objects-explained-for-real-stitch-out-results), [Hatchembroidery – Pull compensation](https://hatchembroidery.com/resources/blog/what-you-need-to-know-about-pull-compensation), software/YouTube guidelines

## Bad pathing / stitch order

**Content from source:** “Poor stitch sequence leads to unnecessary jumps, thread trims, and misalignments. Plan your pathing carefully. Begin from the center and work outward. Stitch from bottom to top. Group similar colors and elements together.” “Incorrect stitch order can cause unnecessary jumps, trims, and thread overlaps. This increases thread tension and raises the risk of thread snapping. Optimized stitch sequencing allows smoother thread flow and fewer machine interruptions.” “Control start/stop points to keep the machine ‘flowing’; jumping across the design wastes time and increases thread-break risk.”

**Source:** [The Embroidery Cloud – Top 7 mistakes](https://theembroiderycloud.com/top-7-common-embroidery-digitizing-mistakes-and-how-to-avoid-them/), [Embroiplanet](https://embroiplanet.com/common-embroidery-problems-caused-by-poor-digitizing-and-how-to-fix-them/), [Hooping Station](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/the-ultimate-embroidery-digitizing-cheat-sheet-density-underlay-pull-comp-and-objects-explained-for-real-stitch-out-results)

## Wrong stitch type

**Content from source:** “A fill stitch on thin lines or a satin stitch on wide areas often results in thread breaks or uneven stitching. Run stitches suit outlines and fine details. Satin stitches are great for text and narrow elements. Fill stitches work well for larger areas.” “Using inappropriate stitch types for borders, fills, or small text can cause rough edges and loss of definition.” “Keep columns between 2 mm and 7–9 mm. Do not go below 1.5 mm. Most algorithms convert satin wider than 12.1 mm to jump stitch.”

**Source:** [The Embroidery Cloud – Top 7 mistakes](https://theembroiderycloud.com/top-7-common-embroidery-digitizing-mistakes-and-how-to-avoid-them/), [Embroiplanet](https://embroiplanet.com/common-embroidery-problems-caused-by-poor-digitizing-and-how-to-fix-them/), [Hooping Station – Satin limits](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/the-ultimate-embroidery-digitizing-cheat-sheet-density-underlay-pull-comp-and-objects-explained-for-real-stitch-out-results)

## Wrong fabric settings

**Content from source:** “A design for canvas may pucker on stretchy knit or get lost on plush fabric. Identify the fabric you’ll use. Adjust your density, underlay, and pull compensation accordingly. Always test on the same fabric type intended for the final product.” “Different fabrics react differently to stitching. Stretch fabrics, thick materials, and textured surfaces all require different stitch approaches. Poor digitizing ignores these differences, leading to distortion and instability. A design digitized for caps may not perform well on t-shirts or jackets.”

**Source:** [The Embroidery Cloud – Top 7 mistakes](https://theembroiderycloud.com/top-7-common-embroidery-digitizing-mistakes-and-how-to-avoid-them/), [Embroiplanet](https://embroiplanet.com/common-embroidery-problems-caused-by-poor-digitizing-and-how-to-fix-them/)

## Stabilizer / hooping

**Content from source:** “You can have perfect underlay digitizing, but if your physical hooping is loose, the design will fail. The fabric must be ‘drum-tight’. If your hands get tired, you might not tighten the hoop enough, causing the design to misalign. Traditional hoop rings must be clamped very tight to hold slippery sportswear or thick hoodies. This often leaves a permanent ‘ring’ (hoop burn) on the fabric. Professional shops use magnetic frames because they clamp firmly without crushing the fabric fibers.”

**Source:** [Hooping Station – Underlay & hooping](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/the-ultimate-embroidery-digitizing-cheat-sheet-density-underlay-pull-comp-and-objects-explained-for-real-stitch-out-results)

## Improper stitch path / distorted shapes

**Content from source:** “Random or poorly planned stitch paths can distort shapes and cause uneven textures. Stitch direction should follow natural design contours to preserve form. Strategic stitch planning improves visual accuracy and structural stability.”

## Insufficient density / gaps

**Content from source:** “Low stitch density can cause fabric to show through, especially on dark fabrics or textured materials. This reduces design clarity and perceived quality. Balanced density ensures full coverage without excessive stiffness.”

**Source:** [Embroiplanet – Common embroidery problems](https://embroiplanet.com/common-embroidery-problems-caused-by-poor-digitizing-and-how-to-fix-them/)

## YouTube and video-based sources

**Content from source (summarized):** Controlling push-pull and fixing gaps requires balancing density and underlay together. Underlay types: Center Line, Edge Run, Zigzag, Tatami; for knits and hoodies, edge runs are generally best. Rather than increasing stitch density to fix coverage, apply appropriate underlay; it increases stitch count but reduces puckering and provides loft so stitches don’t sink into soft fabrics. Pull compensation: 0.2 mm default, 0.4 mm recommended for automatic digitizing; range 0.1–0.4 mm by fabric and density.
