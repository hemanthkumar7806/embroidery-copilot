## Density by fabric (detail with content from sources)

### Cotton

**Content from source:** “Cotton: This fabric loves a standard density (0.4–0.5 mm spacing). It’s great for t-shirts and polos.”

**Source:** [DigitizingUSA – Stitch density](https://www.digitizingusa.com/showblog/stitch-density-in-embroidery-digitizing)

### Fleece

**Content from source:** “Fleece: Use medium density (0.5–0.6 mm) with a light underlay so your stitches don’t get lost in the fuzz.”

**Source:** [DigitizingUSA – Stitch density](https://www.digitizingusa.com/showblog/stitch-density-in-embroidery-digitizing)

### Hoodie (example of thick / stretchy knit)

**Content from source:** “Thick fabric; avoid too dense or design gets stiff. Never pack like cotton.” “For hoodies and stretchy knits: underlay is critical. Without proper underlay, embroidery lies flat and can show through. For knits like hoodies, edge run underlay is often best. Rather than increasing stitch density to fix problems, apply proper underlay instead. For larger areas and stretchy fabrics, more underlay is needed.” “High-pile fabrics (like sherpa fleece or terry cloth): Require more spacing to prevent stiffness.”

**Source:** Community consensus; [Hatchembroidery – Underlay](https://hatchembroidery.com/resources/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-underlay-for-your-machine-embroidery-designs); general best practices.

### Leather

**Content from source:** “Leather: Take it easy with low density (0.6–0.8 mm) to avoid holes. Stick to simple designs to keep it from cracking.”

**Source:** [DigitizingUSA – Stitch density](https://www.digitizingusa.com/showblog/stitch-density-in-embroidery-digitizing)

### Silk / satin (fabric)

**Content from source:** “Silk or Satin: Go light (0.6–0.7 mm) to steer clear of puckering. Underlay stitches help keep things stable.”

**Source:** [DigitizingUSA – Stitch density](https://www.digitizingusa.com/showblog/stitch-density-in-embroidery-digitizing)

### Denim

**Content from source:** “Denim: This heavy material can handle denser stitches (0.3–0.4 mm) for bold designs, but don’t let it get too heavy.”

**Source:** [DigitizingUSA – Stitch density](https://www.digitizingusa.com/showblog/stitch-density-in-embroidery-digitizing)

### Terry cloth / towel

**Content from source:** “Increasing pull compensation values is needed to counteract the thickness and pile of the fabric. The amount of pull compensation should increase incrementally as the thickness of the columns does. Density on your column and fill stitches will need to be increased by about 10% to 20% from standard goods. Zig-zag underlay, even for smaller lettering with thin columns, is advisable to help mat down the terry cloth and not allow the underlay to fall outside the column stitches. Thicker columns over 3mm can use both edge run and zig-zag underlay types. The edge run will help maintain clean edges on your columns. Fill stitches will need more stability within the underlay by using cross-stitched underlay instead of the standard opposite direction underlay. Tear-away backing is advisable; cut-away and solvy can also be used. Using solvy allows much more detail. Rayon thread preferred over polyester for runnability and lifespan.”

**Source:** [Digitizing Made Easy – Terry cloth digitizing](https://www.digitizingmadeeasy.com/embroidering-on-terry-cloth-digitizing-tips-techniques/)

### General numbers (all designs)

## Satin column width (mm)

**Content from source:** “Typical max range (The Sweet Spot): Keep columns between 2 mm and 7–9 mm. Minimum width: Do not go below 1.5 mm. Most commercial algorithms automatically convert any satin stitch wider than 12.1 mm into a ‘Jump’ stitch.”

**Source:** [Hooping Station – Digitizing cheat sheet](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/the-ultimate-embroidery-digitizing-cheat-sheet-density-underlay-pull-comp-and-objects-explained-for-real-stitch-out-results)

## Underlay by fabric and size

**Content from source:** “Stable fabric (denim, canvas, twill): Standard tear-away or cut-away; basic Edge Run or Zigzag underlay. Stretchy/unstable (t-shirts, polos, knits): Increase underlay (Zigzag + Edge Run); use cut-away stabilizer (e.g. no-show mesh). Narrow satin (&lt; 3 mm): Light underlay (Center Run) to avoid bulk and needle breakage. Large areas: Heavy support (Double Zigzag + Edge Run) to prevent rippling. Larger areas and stretchy fabrics such as knits and pique generally need more underlay than smaller areas and firm fabrics like drill or leather. On knits, edge runs are best. When you want a full design with extra lift under the stitches, a tatami underlay is preferable.”

**Source:** [Hooping Station](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/the-ultimate-embroidery-digitizing-cheat-sheet-density-underlay-pull-comp-and-objects-explained-for-real-stitch-out-results), [Hatchembroidery – Underlay](https://hatchembroidery.com/resources/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-underlay-for-your-machine-embroidery-designs)

## Pull compensation

**Content from source:** “A standard starting point is often 0.17 mm - 0.20 mm. Never digitize ‘line to line.’ If you want two shapes to touch, they must overlap in the software.”

**Source:** [Hooping Station – Digitizing cheat sheet](https://hoopingstation.com/blogs/articles/the-ultimate-embroidery-digitizing-cheat-sheet-density-underlay-pull-comp-and-objects-explained-for-real-stitch-out-results)

## YouTube / video-based

**Content from source (summarized):** For wearable items, satin spacing 1.5 mm to 5.7 mm; minimum stitch length 0.5 mm; safe zone 1 mm. Vinyl or leather require less density to avoid puncturing holes; high-pile fabrics (sherpa fleece, terry cloth) need more spacing. Underlay: large areas heavy support, medium/wide columns edge run or zigzag, very narrow columns center run only.
